## Abstract

Public retail forecasting benchmarks rank methods by forecast error, although forecasts are used to decide how much to stock. We embed eight probabilistic forecasting methods in one transparent order-up-to policy with lost sales and optional liquidation:

- three statistical models;
- four gradient-boosting models;
- a deep-learning model (N-HiTS).

We evaluate them on two public datasets from different retail settings, M5 (brick-and-mortar) and VN1 (multi-vendor e-commerce). We keep every eligible series and repeat all experiments on three 26-week test windows. Methods are compared at equal realised fill rate along their inventory–fill-rate trade-off curves, with bootstrap confidence intervals. A cost view then checks the conclusions for lost-sales penalties of 10 to 1,000 weeks of holding cost.

On M5, LightGBM quantile regression (LGB-Q) is the most accurate method. At a fill rate of 0.94 it needs 3–11% less inventory than point forecasts with safety stock, conformal quantiles, TSB and exponential smoothing, in every window, and also at lead times of one and four weeks (checked in the main window).

On VN1, the most inventory-efficient method changes between windows. We trace this to demand dynamics. A negative-binomial TSB model needs 13–16% less inventory on series with stable demand, whereas only the global quantile model serves series whose demand grows sharply. Neither the ADI–CV² classes nor an unsupervised segmentation separates these series in advance. A classifier that predicts growth recovers part of the possible gain, but unstably.

In a post hoc analysis, averaging the quantiles of LGB-Q and TSB-NB hedges between the two. The average needs 6–11% less inventory than LGB-Q on VN1 at a fill rate of 0.92 in all three windows, stays within 1.5% of it on M5, and has the smallest worst-case regret (0.9% against 9.4%). This holds for lost-sales penalties up to 200 weeks of holding. For larger penalties, the most accurate method is the cheapest.

N-HiTS is well calibrated but leads in no window. A quantile-based liquidation rule costs almost no service, but under the assumed costs no rule pays off within 26 weeks.

**Keywords:** probabilistic forecasting; inventory management; intermittent demand; quantile regression; forecast combination; demand segmentation; M5; VN1

## 1 Introduction

Retailers face two opposite risks. With too little stock they lose sales; with too much they tie up capital and eventually have to clear products at a loss. Setting stock levels requires the uncertainty of demand, not only its expected value, so quantile forecasts are the natural input to inventory decisions [3].

Public benchmarks have driven progress in probabilistic demand forecasting, but they score forecasts, not decisions [24].

- The M5 competition used the weighted RMSSE and the weighted scaled pinball loss [1], and its organisers state that it did not target a specific decision problem [3].
- Studies on the more recent VN1 e-commerce data compare accuracy and computational cost [4].

Yet higher accuracy does not necessarily give better inventory decisions [5, 6, 23]:

- On M5, Spiliotis et al. [23] find that simple empirical quantile methods give better inventory performance than machine learning in a 28-day simulation.
- Theodorou et al. [7] report that the most accurate method may not be the best choice when lost-sales costs dominate, especially for intermittent products and short review periods and lead times.
- Theodorou et al. [7] call for replications on online retail data, on shorter series and with neural probabilistic forecasts.

Four gaps remain.

1. **Series selection.** Studies that link forecasts to inventory on M5 often keep only selected series, such as 8,000 high-volume series [8], a subset of volatile food items [9] or series with at least 2.5 years of history [7], although most retail series are intermittent.
2. **One dataset, one test period.** They rely on one dataset and one test period [5, 7, 8, 9, 23], so it is unknown whether their conclusions transfer to another retail setting or another period. Theodorou et al. [7] themselves caution that a simulated year gives no guarantee for the next.
3. **Assumed costs.** Public datasets contain neither costs nor inventory positions, so cost-based evaluations depend on assumed parameters, and cost-based and cost-free conclusions can disagree [7].
4. **No guidance when the best method changes.** When the best method changes from one period to the next, studies offer no guidance on what to use when one cannot tell in advance which method will win.

This paper addresses these gaps with a forecast-to-decision benchmark. It does not propose a new forecasting model. Our contributions are:

1. **A reproducible benchmark** of eight probabilistic forecasting methods inside one transparent replenishment-and-liquidation policy, on two public retail datasets from different settings, with all eligible series retained (those with at least 13 weeks of history and one sale before the test period). It runs on a laptop CPU.
2. **A cost-free evaluation protocol.** Methods are compared at equal realised fill rate along their trade-off curves, with bootstrap confidence intervals. A cost view then maps the same curves to total cost over a wide range of lost-sales penalties.
3. **Evidence on robustness.** Every experiment is repeated on three test windows; the main comparison is also repeated under three lead times and four hyper-parameter settings. This separates findings that hold everywhere from those that depend on the period, the policy or the configuration.
4. **An explanation of the period dependence on e-commerce data.** We show that it is associated with how much demand grows in the test period. Neither the standard demand classes nor an unsupervised segmentation anticipates this, and a supervised growth predictor helps only partly.
5. **A simple hedge.** The average of the quantiles of the global quantile model and TSB-NB, identified post hoc, has the smallest worst-case regret in the six dataset–window runs tested, unless lost sales are very expensive. It needs confirmation on new periods.

The rest of the paper is organised as follows:

- Section 2 reviews related work.
- Sections 3 and 4 describe the method and the experimental setup.
- Section 5 presents the results.
- Section 6 discusses implications and limitations.
- Section 7 concludes.

## 2 Related Work

### 2.1 Retail forecasting benchmarks

- **M5 accuracy track.** LightGBM was used by all of the top 50 teams, and the winner combined LightGBM models trained with a Tweedie loss [2].
- **M5 uncertainty track.** The winner trained a separate LightGBM model for each quantile [3].

These results motivate our main model and our point-forecast baseline. Zanotti [4] used M5 and VN1 to study the cost of ensembling global models; that study evaluates accuracy and computational cost, not inventory outcomes.

### 2.2 Intermittent demand

- **TSB.** The TSB method updates a demand probability every period, which lets it react to obsolescence [10].
- **ADI–CV² classes.** Demand is commonly classified by the average inter-demand interval (ADI) and the squared coefficient of variation of demand sizes (CV²) [11]; the thresholds 4/3 and 0.5 are used to describe M5 [1].
- **Negative binomial.** The negative binomial is a common distribution for intermittent demand in stock control [25].
- **Global vs local models.** Damato et al. [12] compared local and global probabilistic models on intermittent data and concluded that no global architecture is established for such series.

### 2.3 From forecasts to inventory decisions

A structured review observes that most forecasting studies ignore the step from forecast to replenishment decision [6].

**Using inventory outcomes to fit or evaluate forecasts:**
- Kourentzes et al. [13] tune exponential smoothing with an inventory-based cost function and question whether forecast accuracy is a reasonable proxy for the decisions it supports.
- van der Haar et al. [14] learn order quantities end to end.
- Petropoulos et al. [24] simulate an order-up-to policy over rolling origins on 334 monthly M3 series with lead times of 1 to 12 months, and find that forecast combinations give the best inventory performance.
- Babai et al. [25] use efficiency curves of achieved service level against inventory cost on 4,815 slow-moving items, and show that temporal aggregation raises achieved service levels.

**Studies on M5:**
- Spiliotis et al. [23] compare 21 probabilistic methods on the 30,490 product–store series over one 28-day period, with daily review and a one-day lead time. Simple empirical quantile methods give better inventory performance than the competition winners, which are poorly calibrated at low service levels.
- Wang et al. [5] evaluate probabilistic forecast combinations with a newsvendor rule in a single-period setting, which they list as a limitation.
- Zabraoui et al. [9] simulate inventory with reinforcement-learning policies on selected items.
- Mohammed et al. [8] evaluate safety stock with one illustrative cost calculation on high-volume series.

**The closest study** is Theodorou et al. [7]:
- *Design.* They simulate one year of an order-up-to policy with lost sales on 7,783 daily product–state series of M5. They use 13 methods, whose quantiles are point forecasts plus empirical quantiles of past errors, under 24 combinations of target service level (90/95/99%), review period (3–21 days) and lead time (3 or 9 days).
- *Accuracy.* Temporal-aggregation methods are the most accurate, and LightGBM only at the longest horizon.
- *Trade-off curves.* The curves broadly follow accuracy.
- *Costs.* Total costs with a lost-sales penalty much larger than the holding cost favour less accurate methods, whose inflated quantiles hold more stock.
- *Calibration.* Realised service levels differ from the targets and between methods.

### 2.4 Method selection and combination

**Combination.** Combining forecasts is a long-standing way to hedge against model uncertainty, and Petropoulos et al. [24] find that combinations give the best inventory outcomes. For probabilistic forecasts, the weighted average of the members' quantile functions is called quantile averaging or Vincentization; it tends to give a sharper combined distribution than averaging probabilities (linear pooling) [26]. The review of [26] also documents the "forecast combination puzzle": the simple equal-weight average often outperforms sophisticated weighting schemes that should, in theory, be superior.

**Selection by series features.** FFORMA [27] trains a meta-model on features of each series to assign combination weights that minimise the forecast loss, and it outperformed a simple combination and the individual methods in the M4 competition. Our segmentation (Section 5.4), growth-prediction rule (Section 5.5) and learned combination weight (Section 5.6) are simple instances of this idea. Unlike [27], we evaluate them by the inventory they require rather than by forecast loss.

### 2.5 Positioning

Our study differs from these works in combining:
- two retail settings;
- all demand classes;
- a liquidation decision;
- three test windows;
- a deep-learning baseline.

Compared with [7], we also:
- use direct quantile models;
- compare methods at equal realised fill rate rather than at equal target service level;
- map the same curves to costs over a range of penalties instead of one cost setting.

Multi-period simulation [7, 9, 14, 24] and decision-level evaluation [5, 7, 23] are not new in themselves.

## 3 Method

### 3.1 Data

**M5** [1] contains daily unit sales of 3,049 products in 10 Walmart stores (30,490 product–store series). We aggregate them to 277 complete weeks.

**VN1** [21] is a weekly e-commerce dataset with 15,053 client–warehouse–product series over 196 weeks; despite its name it is not Vietnamese data.

A series is evaluated if:
- it starts at least 13 weeks before the test period; and
- it has at least one sale before the test period.

The datasets differ clearly even in demand behaviour alone (AUC 0.91), and stably across windows. VN1 series are:
- shorter;
- more intermittent;
- more volatile in trend;
- often dormant: a quarter of them had no sale in the 15 weeks before the test period.

Section 5.9 examines whether these differences explain our results.

### 3.2 Forecasting target

At each weekly origin o, every method forecasts the quantiles q ∈ {0.5, 0.8, 0.9, 0.95, 0.99} of the cumulative demand D_h(o) = Y[o] + … + Y[o+h−1] directly:
- h = 3 for replenishment (lead time plus review period);
- h = 13 for liquidation.

Weekly quantiles are not summed, because the quantile of a sum is not the sum of quantiles. Forecasting the total over the protection interval directly is a form of temporal aggregation, which raises achieved service levels for intermittent demand [25].

### 3.3 Methods

**Statistical methods (local, one model per series):**
- **EMP**: empirical quantiles of rolling h-week sums over the last 104 weeks.
- **ETS**: simple exponential smoothing with a normal distribution for the h-week total [18].
- **TSB-NB**: TSB [10] with a negative-binomial distribution matched to the mean and variance of the h-week total.

**Global gradient-boosting methods (one model for all series):**
- **LGB-T**: a LightGBM [15] point model with a Tweedie loss plus a normal safety stock from each series' validation residuals. This is the common "point forecast plus safety stock" practice.
- **LGB-C**: the same point model with split-conformal quantiles [17] pooled by demand class.
- **HGB-Q**: histogram gradient boosting [19] with a quantile loss, trained on a 300,000-row sample; a check that the results do not depend on one library.
- **LGB-Q**: LightGBM quantile regression [16], one global model per quantile (main model).

**Deep learning:**
- **N-HiTS**: a global N-HiTS network [28], a multilayer-perceptron architecture with hierarchical interpolation and multi-rate input sampling, from the neuralforecast library, trained with a multi-quantile loss on the series of forward h-week sums. D_h(o) is the h-th step of an h-step-ahead forecast of that series, so its quantiles are again forecast directly. The model uses an input window of 52 weeks and 1,500 training steps; it is univariate, untuned and uses one seed.

**Combinations (Section 5.6):**
- **C2**: the mean of the LGB-Q and TSB-NB quantiles.
- **C3**: the mean of the LGB-Q, HGB-Q and TSB-NB quantiles.

**Shared design of the four boosting models:**
- *Features:* lags, rolling means and standard deviations, zero share, weeks since the last sale, calendar, price, and a scale. On M5 they add events, SNAP days and the product hierarchy.
- *Target:* scaled by the series level.
- *Hyper-parameters:* fixed (learning rate 0.05, 63 leaves, at least 200 samples per leaf, L2 penalty 1, feature fraction 0.8); Section 5.7 varies them.

### 3.4 Decision layer

Each week, for every series:
1. the order placed L weeks earlier arrives;
2. the inventory position IP is computed as on-hand plus on-order stock;
3. stock above a liquidation threshold T is liquidated;
4. an order max(S − IP, 0) is placed, with S the τ-quantile of demand over L + 1 weeks, unless stock was liquidated;
5. demand occurs, and unmet demand is lost.

The default lead time is L = 2 weeks with weekly review; Section 5.7 uses L = 1 and 4.

Four liquidation policies use the same replenishment forecasts:
- **none**;
- **quantile**: T is the 0.95-quantile of 13-week demand from the same method;
- **fixed**: T is 26 times the mean weekly sales of the last 26 weeks;
- **dead13**: a series without a sale in 13 weeks is liquidated completely and not replenished until it sells again.

### 3.5 Evaluation

**Accuracy.** The usual scaled quantile loss (SQL) divides each series' pinball loss by its in-sample naive error. Its mean is unstable on VN1 (Section 5.1). We therefore report:
- the volume-weighted quantile loss wQL (total pinball loss divided by total demand);
- the empirical coverage of each quantile;
- the median per-series SQL;
- mean per-series ranks with the Friedman test and the Nemenyi critical difference [20].

**Inventory at equal fill rate.** Over the 22 test weeks after a 4-week warm-up we compute:
- the fill rate (units sold / units demanded);
- inventory in weeks of demand.

At a fixed τ the methods reach different fill rates. We therefore:
1. run τ over the five quantile levels;
2. draw each method's trade-off curve, a standard tool in inventory evaluations [7, 24, 25];
3. interpolate the inventory needed to reach a target fill rate.

Two summary measures follow from the curves:
- **Frontier rank:** a method's mean rank of required inventory over the targets 0.90–0.98.
- **Regret:** a method's excess inventory over the best single method in a run (dataset and window), averaged over the targets it reaches. We report the maximum regret over runs.

Confidence intervals come from resampling series (200 resamples, seed 2026).

**Cost view.** With unit cost c, weekly holding rate h_w and a lost-sales penalty b per unit, the cost per unit of demand is c·(h_w·inventory + b·(1 − fill)). Dividing by c·h_w gives the cost in weeks of holding:

C(r) = inventory_weeks + r·(1 − fill_rate), with r = b / h_w.

Each method is allowed its cheapest τ, and r runs from 10 to 1,000. For reference:
- a 20% annual holding rate with a 30% lost margin gives r ≈ 78;
- the cost parameters of [7] give r ≈ 650.

**Liquidation.** For each policy we compute the break-even salvage ratio s*: the minimum salvage price per unit cost at which liquidating is at least as profitable as holding. It is computed over holding costs of 10–40% per year and gross margins of 30–100%.

## 4 Experimental Setup

**Test windows.**
- The last 26 weeks of a panel form the test period, in two 13-week blocks.
- Models are retrained at the start of each block, and every test week is a forecast origin.
- To test robustness we drop the last 26 and the last 52 weeks and repeat everything, including model training, on the preceding 26 weeks.
- This gives three test windows per dataset: main, second and third.

**Hardware and run times** (laptop CPU: Intel Core i5-12450H, 16 GB RAM):

| Method | M5 | VN1 |
|---|---|---|
| LGB-Q, one horizon | 1,251 s | 146 s |
| TSB-NB | 68 s | 30 s |
| N-HiTS, one window, h = 3 | 7 min (second laptop, 20 threads, 16 GB) | 5–6 min |

## 5 Results

### 5.1 Accuracy

*[Table 3 = v3_tables.md Table 2, plus an N-HiTS row: wQL M5 0.078 / 0.083 / 0.089, VN1 0.107 / 0.079 / 0.104 (source `code/outputs/neural_eval.md`)]*

**Volume-weighted loss.** By wQL, LGB-Q is the most accurate method in both datasets and all three windows, with HGB-Q within 0.002. LGB-Q is also the best method in every demand class of both datasets and every window.

**N-HiTS** ranks behind LGB-Q in every run, but ahead of TSB-NB:
- on VN1 its wQL is 0.107, 0.079 and 0.104 in the three windows, against 0.103, 0.078 and 0.102 for LGB-Q and 0.129, 0.088 and 0.138 for TSB-NB;
- on M5 its wQL is 0.078, 0.083 and 0.089, against 0.076, 0.080 and 0.086 for LGB-Q and 0.088, 0.091 and 0.098 for TSB-NB;
- it is well calibrated in the upper tail: the empirical coverage of the 0.99 quantile is 0.986–0.992 on VN1 and 0.987–0.988 on M5.

**Per-series ranks.**
- On M5, the per-series ranks agree with wQL: LGB-Q ranks first at h = 3 in every window, ahead of HGB-Q by more than the critical difference.
- On VN1 the per-series view differs.
  - At h = 3, LGB-Q, HGB-Q and TSB-NB are within 0.17 mean ranks, and LGB-Q and HGB-Q differ by less than the critical difference.
  - At h = 13, TSB-NB ranks first in all three windows (mean rank 2.92 / 2.99 / 3.37 against 4.02 / 3.48 / 3.85 for LGB-Q).

The global models are thus not more accurate than TSB-NB for a typical VN1 series; their advantage in total loss comes from avoiding rare, very large errors.

**Mean SQL is misleading.** For LGB-Q on VN1 the mean SQL is 0.336, 0.539 and 2.008 in the three windows, driven by a few series with a tiny naive error, whereas wQL is 0.103, 0.078 and 0.102.

**M5.**
- LGB-Q has the best frontier rank in every resample of every window.
- At a fill rate of 0.94 it needs 3.3–10.6% less inventory than LGB-T, LGB-C, TSB-NB and ETS, and every interval excludes zero.

- Direct quantiles therefore beat the point forecast plus safety stock (LGB-T) by 3.3–6.8%.

**VN1.** The answer depends on the window.
- *Main window:* LGB-Q has the best frontier rank, but its lead over LGB-T, LGB-C and TSB-NB at a fill rate of 0.94 is not significant.
- *Second window:* TSB-NB leads, and TSB-NB, LGB-T and LGB-C need 4.1–8.8% less inventory than LGB-Q.

**N-HiTS** does not lead in any VN1 window. Relative to LGB-Q, at a fill rate of 0.92 it needs:
- +8.3% [+3.6, +12.7] in the main window;
- −2.1% [−8.3, +0.4] in the second;
- +2.6% [−1.8, +10.8] in the third.

Its frontier rank among the eight single methods is 4.4, 2.8 and 3.8. On M5 it needs 2.6–10.0% more inventory than LGB-Q at fill rates 0.90–0.94 in every window, with every interval above zero, and its frontier rank is 3.8, 2.6 and 5.8; it is less efficient than HGB-Q but more efficient than TSB-NB in two of three windows.

**By demand class** *[Table 5 = v3_tables.md Table 4]*:
- On M5, LGB-Q leads in the smooth, intermittent and lumpy classes in every window.
- On VN1 only the lumpy class has the same leader (LGB-Q) in all three windows; the smooth class is led by TSB-NB in two windows and by LGB-C in the third.

### 5.3 Why VN1 depends on the period

*[Table 6 = v3_tables.md Table 5]*

We split the series of each window by how their demand changed between the 26 weeks before the test period and the test period itself:
- **stable:** 0.67–1.5 times;
- **growing:** more than 1.5 times.

This split uses realised demand, so it is descriptive and cannot guide decisions.

The pattern is the same in all three windows:
- On VN1 series with stable demand, TSB-NB needs 13.3–15.6% less inventory than LGB-Q.
- On series whose demand grows, TSB-NB fails to reach a fill rate of 0.92 in two windows, whereas LGB-Q reaches it.
- The aggregate result follows the share of demand that comes from growing series: 31.5% and 40.4% in the windows where LGB-Q leads, and 22.8% in the window where TSB-NB leads.
- In the third window the demand of the intermittent class is 3.2 times that of the previous half-year, and two thirds of it comes from series that grew more than tenfold.
- On M5, LGB-Q needs less inventory in both strata in every window.

### 5.4 Can a better grouping of series anticipate this?

If growth were predictable from a series' history, a method could be chosen per group of series. We tested two groupings: the ADI–CV² classes, and an unsupervised segmentation built for this purpose.

**Building the segmentation.**
- *Features:* eight computed before the test period: level, order size, ADI, CV², weeks since the last sale, 26-week trend, recent level relative to history, and peak size relative to order size.
- *Fitting:* on the earliest window, then applied unchanged to the later ones.
- *Candidates:* K-means and Gaussian mixtures with 3 to 8 components.
- *Selection criteria, fixed in advance:* bootstrap stability (adjusted Rand index ≥ 0.70) and a minimum cluster size of 3% in both datasets; then the highest silhouette.

**What it found.**
- A three-component Gaussian mixture was selected. It yields a regular segment (68–70% of the M5 series), a fading segment (24–26%) and a sparse segment (6–7%).
- It is stable (adjusted Rand index 0.94 between refits).
- It differs from the ADI–CV² classes (adjusted Rand index 0.19–0.22 on M5 and 0.39–0.41 on VN1). The fading segment cuts across all four classes.
- The clusters are not well separated: the silhouette is 0.21, and HDBSCAN labels 43% of the series as noise. The series form a continuum, and the segmentation divides it rather than discovering natural groups.

**Explanatory power.** The segments explain more of the per-series accuracy difference between LGB-Q and TSB-NB than the classes in 11 of 12 cases (η² of the log SQL ratio up to 18.2% against 14.3% on VN1). Both groupings explain less than a fifth of it.

*[Table 7 = v3_tables.md Table 6]*

**Neither grouping helps to choose a method.** Consider a rule that assigns each group the method that was most inventory-efficient for it in the previous window:
- On M5 it stays within 0.3% of LGB-Q.
- On VN1, with the ADI–CV² classes, it saves 5.6% in one transition and tends to cost 5.1% in the other.
- On VN1, with the segments, it saves nothing and then costs 10.5%.

Even an oracle that knows the best method of each segment in the test window does no better than the best single method of that window, because within a window the same method leads in every segment.

The reason is that the share of series that will grow is almost the same in every group: 18–31% in the three segments and 14–39% in the four ADI–CV² classes on VN1.

### 5.5 Predicting growth directly

Since the groupings do not separate growing from stable series, we tried to predict growth directly. The rule is learned entirely on earlier windows and then applied, unchanged, to the next window:

1. Label each series of the training window(s) as growing if its test demand exceeds 1.5 times that of the previous 26 weeks.
2. Fit a gradient-boosting classifier on nine pre-test features.
3. Choose method A, the most efficient for growing series, and method B, the most efficient for the others.
4. Choose the share of series sent to A that minimises the inventory of the composite policy at a fill rate of 0.92, using out-of-fold probabilities.

There are three transitions: third → second, second → main, and (third + second) → main.

**What the rule learns.**
- On VN1 it always assigns TSB-NB to the series predicted to be stable and a global model to those predicted to grow.
- On M5 it collapses to LGB-Q, and every M5 rule is within 0.3% of LGB-Q.
- The out-of-sample AUC of the growth classifier on VN1 is only 0.66–0.70.

**What the rule achieves.**
- An oracle that knows which series grow would need 5.6–9.8% less inventory than LGB-Q in both evaluation windows, so growth is the right axis for choosing a method.
- The learned rule recovers part of this: 5.2–7.0% less inventory in the second window, and 5.2% less at a fill rate of 0.92 in the main window when trained on two windows.
- Trained on a single window it selects too small a share for the growth method and becomes nearly TSB-NB (+9.2% at 0.94).

**Sensitivity to the share sent to A.** On the next window, at a fill rate of 0.92, the result changes with the share as follows (third → second / second → main / two windows → main):
- share 5%: −10.7 / +3.0 / −2.7%;
- share 30%: −9.7 / −0.3 / −4.5%;
- share 70%: −7.0 / −0.4 / −5.2%.

The rule is therefore promising but unstable, and Section 5.6 shows a simpler alternative.

### 5.6 A combination hedges between the leaders

LGB-Q and TSB-NB fail on different series of VN1: TSB-NB under-covers series that grow, and LGB-Q holds too much stock for stable series. We therefore average their quantiles (C2). This needs no prediction of growth. The pair was chosen after observing that the two methods alternate as leaders, so this analysis is not fully out of sample. The secondary combination C3, which adds HGB-Q, was specified at the same time.

### 5.9 Are the differences between the datasets artefacts?

We checked three possible artefacts (details in `06_experiment_results/phase2_b1_b2.md`).

1. **Composition.** Re-weighting the VN1 series by inverse propensity so that their feature distribution matches M5 reduces the mean standardised difference from 0.32–0.39 to 0.02–0.03. It leaves the VN1 conclusions unchanged:
   - LGB-Q is more accurate than TSB-NB on 49–51% of the re-weighted series, against 60–64% on M5;
   - TSB-NB still leads in the second window.
2. **Prices.** VN1 prices are observed in less than half of the weeks. Refitting the four global models without price features changes the inventory needed by LGB-Q by less than 1% in every window.
3. **Information.** The global models on M5 use six features that VN1 lacks. Refitting them on M5 with the 21 shared features changes the inventory needed by LGB-Q by less than 1% in every window. LGB-Q keeps the best frontier rank in every resample, and still needs 4.2–9.0% less inventory than TSB-NB and 2.7–6.6% less than LGB-T.

None of the three checks removes the difference between the datasets, so we attribute it to the retail settings themselves rather than to data preparation.

**Service cost.** With LGB-Q forecasts:
- the quantile rule costs 0.02 percentage points of fill rate or less in every window and dataset;
- on M5 it almost never triggers; on VN1 it reduces inventory by 1.7–7.4%;
- the fixed rule and the dead-stock rule lose more service: they raise the stockout rate by 5–20% and 10–23%;

## 6 Discussion

### 6.1 Compare at equal service level, over several periods

At τ = 0.9 the methods reach fill rates between 0.935 and 0.967 on M5, because each calibrates its quantiles differently. Comparing inventory at equal τ rewards methods that under-cover; trade-off curves remove this effect without a cost assumption. Theodorou et al. [7] observe the same mismatch between target and realised service levels.

A single test period would have given a confident but wrong answer on VN1 in either direction, which bears out the caution of [7] that a simulated year gives no guarantee for the next.

### 6.2 Direct quantiles matter

Many studies, including [7] and several methods of [23], derive quantiles from point forecasts and past errors. On M5 this costs 3.3–8.2% more inventory at a fill rate of 0.94 than direct quantiles from the same LightGBM model (LGB-T and LGB-C in Table 4).

This, besides the daily product–state level, may explain why [7] found LightGBM less accurate than simpler methods except at the longest horizon. Similarly, simple empirical methods won in the 28-day daily simulation of [23], whereas empirical quantiles (EMP) are among the least efficient methods in our weekly evaluation over 26 weeks. We read these differences as effects of the frequency, the evaluation length and the quantile construction, not as contradictions; our design cannot separate the three.

### 6.3 On e-commerce data the best method depends on what demand will do

**The pattern.** A well-specified statistical model is the more economical choice for series that continue as before, and a global quantile model is the only one that copes with series that take off. This is consistent with [7], who find on M5 that the most accurate method need not be the best when lost-sales costs dominate, especially for intermittent products. Our results add two things:
- the divergence depends on the period;
- it is not visible on M5 at the service levels we studied.

**Predicting growth.** Growth is the right axis for method selection: an oracle on growth saves 5.6–9.8% on VN1. But growth is hard to predict (AUC 0.66–0.70 out of sample), and neither demand classes nor a segmentation capture it.

**What practitioners can do.** Averaging the two methods is the practical answer: it obtains savings of the same size as the learned rule, without any prediction, in every window. This agrees with the evidence that combinations perform well in inventory settings [24]. Learning the weight (Table 9b), like learning a switching rule (Table 8), does not beat the equal-weight average on VN1. Both are instances of the forecast combination puzzle [26], and they suggest that feature-based weighting such as [27] needs more training periods than our three windows to pay off at the decision level.

### 6.4 Which method to use

**If the lost-sales penalty is moderate** (up to about 200 weeks of holding cost per lost unit):
- C2 had the smallest worst-case regret in our six runs. Its worst-case excess over the best single method is 0.9% in inventory and 3.1% in cost, against 9.4% and 8.1% for LGB-Q.
- On brick-and-mortar data the choice matters little: C2 and LGB-Q differ by less than 2%.
- On e-commerce data C2 saves 6–11%.

**If lost sales are very expensive:**
- the method that reaches the highest fill rate wins, which is LGB-Q;
- unlike in [7], expensive lost sales then favour the most accurate method, because here the less accurate TSB-NB under-covers its upper quantiles instead of inflating them.

**Among the global models on VN1**, the order of LGB-Q, LGB-C and HGB-Q is not robust: they change places when the price features are removed.

### 6.5 Deep learning

A standard univariate N-HiTS is competitive in accuracy on VN1 and well calibrated, but it does not lead in inventory efficiency in any window and does not change any conclusion.

This answers part of the call of [7] for neural probabilistic forecasts. It does not establish an upper bound for deep learning: the model uses no covariates and no tuning. On M5, where LGB-Q also uses prices, events and the product hierarchy, N-HiTS needs 2.6–10.0% more inventory than LGB-Q in every window, whereas on VN1 the comparison is mixed and N-HiTS is slightly ahead in the second window.

### 6.6 Limitations

- **Demand data.** Sales are used as demand, so historical stockouts are not visible.
- **Simulation assumptions.** Unmet demand is lost and liquidation does not change demand. Pack sizes, minimum order quantities and capacity limits, which [7] name as relevant constraints, are not modelled.
- **Ex post analysis.** The demand-shift analysis is ex post.
- **Choice of the combination.** C2 was specified after the main analysis, so its regret is an in-sample result over the three windows. It is evidence for the hedge, not a guarantee of its performance in deployment, and should be confirmed on new periods and datasets.
- **Few windows.** Bootstrap intervals reflect variation across series, not across independent periods. Statements about stability therefore refer to the three windows tested. Three windows per dataset are few, and method rankings agree only weakly between the two datasets (Spearman correlation of frontier ranks 0.86, 0.29 and 0.61).
- **Lead-time check.** The lead-time check covers the main window only.
- **Chronos-2.** A zero-shot, univariate use of the Chronos-2 foundation model [22] on VN1 was less accurate than LGB-Q on 69–73% of the series and never the most inventory-efficient method (Appendix A).
- **Vietnamese case study.** A case study on Vietnamese footwear retail data with actual unit costs gave similar break-even salvage ratios but has unresolved data-quality and licence issues, so it is not reported.

## 7 Conclusion

We benchmarked eight probabilistic forecasting methods and two combinations inside one replenishment-and-liquidation policy on two public retail datasets, three test windows, three lead times, four hyper-parameter settings and a range of cost structures.

- **Brick-and-mortar data (M5).** The conclusion holds in all three test windows: LightGBM quantile regression is the most accurate and the most inventory-efficient single method.
- **E-commerce data (VN1).** The best single method changes with the period, because a statistical model is more economical for stable series while only a global quantile model serves series whose demand grows sharply. Neither demand classes nor a segmentation anticipate which series will grow, and a supervised predictor helps only partly.
- **Combination.** Averaging the quantiles of LightGBM and TSB-NB hedges between them. In a post hoc analysis, it needs 6–11% less inventory than LightGBM on VN1 at a fill rate of 0.92 in all three windows, stays within 1.5% of it on M5, and has the smallest worst-case regret unless lost sales are very expensive.
- **Deep learning.** A standard N-HiTS does not change these conclusions.
- **Liquidation.** A quantile-based liquidation rule is safe for service, but under the assumed costs liquidation pays off within 26 weeks only at salvage prices near unit cost.

Future work should:
- test covariate-informed neural and foundation models;
- learn combination weights per series;
- extend the horizon for liquidation;
- add logistic constraints to the policy.
